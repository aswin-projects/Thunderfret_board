1 The Staff, Clefs & Note Names

Music is written on a staff — 5 horizontal lines and 4 spaces. A clef at the beginning tells you which notes the lines and spaces represent.

Treble Clef (𝄞) — The G Clef

Used for higher notes: guitar, violin, flute, and the right hand of piano. The curl circles the G line (2nd line).

Lines (bottom→top): E–G–B–D–F → "Every Good Boy Deserves Favor"
Spaces (bottom→top): F–A–C–E → "FACE" (just like your face!)

Bass Clef (𝄢) — The F Clef

Used for lower notes: bass guitar, cello, trombone, and the left hand of piano. The two dots surround the F line.

Lines (bottom→top): G–B–D–F–A → "Good Boys Do Fine Always"
Spaces (bottom→top): A–C–E–G → "All Cows Eat Grass"

The Grand Staff (Piano)

Piano music uses both clefs joined by a brace. Middle C (C4) sits on a short ledger line between them.

3 Time Signatures

The two numbers at the start of a piece are the time signature. They tell musicians how to count each measure (bar).

4
4

Common Time

4 beats per bar. Quarter note = 1 beat. Used in pop, rock, classical.

3
4

Waltz Time

3 beats per bar. Strong–weak–weak. Used in waltzes and minuets.

2
4

March Time

2 beats per bar. Strong–weak. Used in marches and polkas.

6
8

Compound Duple

6 beats per bar. Eighth note = 1 beat. Flowing feel like a jig.

Top number = how many beats in each bar.
Bottom number = which note gets one beat (4 = quarter note, 8 = eighth note, 2 = half note).

4 Key Signatures for Trinity Grades 1–3

A key signature is a group of sharps (♯) or flats (♭) at the beginning of every staff. It tells us which scale the piece uses.

C Major / A Minor

No sharps or flats. The easiest key to start with.

G Major / E Minor

One sharp: F♯. Remember: Father Charles...

D Major / B Minor

Two sharps: F♯, C♯. Father Charles...

F Major / D Minor

One flat: B♭. Remember: Battle Ends...

Memory trick for sharps: Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle (F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯, B♯).
For flats: Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father (B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, C♭, F♭).

5 Reading Music for Guitar

Guitar music is written in the treble clef. But one note on the staff can be played in many places on the guitar! Beginners usually start in open position (first 4 frets).

Open Position Notes

E4 → 1st string open
F4 → 1st string 1st fret
G4 → 1st string 3rd fret
B4 → 2nd string open
C5 → 2nd string 1st fret
D5 → 2nd string 3rd fret
C4 → 2nd string 1st fret (lower octave)

Tips for Guitarists

• Always check the key signature first
• Look for open strings — they ring clearly
• Use the same fret for notes close together
• Count lines/spaces from a note you know

6 Reading Music for Piano

Pianists read two staves at once — treble (right hand) and bass (left hand). Middle C is the anchor point between them.

Right Hand (Treble): Thumb on Middle C. Fingers 1-2-3-4-5 cover C-D-E-F-G.
Left Hand (Bass): Thumb on Middle C (finger 1). Fingers 5-4-3-2-1 cover C-B-A-G-F below.

10 Trinity Exam Preparation Checklist

Grade 1 Requirements

  • Know the notes in the C major scale (C–D–E–F–G–A–B)
  • Read simple rhythms in 4/4 and 3/4 time
  • Recognize whole, half, quarter notes and rests
  • Play a simple piece from memory or sight-read 4–8 bars
  • Understand basic dynamics: piano (p) and forte (f)

Grade 2 Requirements

  • Add G major (1 sharp: F♯) and F major (1 flat: B♭)
  • Read eighth-note rhythms and simple ties
  • Understand key signatures and time signatures
  • Sight-read melodies with stepwise motion and small leaps
  • Know basic Italian terms: legato, staccato, crescendo, diminuendo

Grade 3 Requirements

  • Add D major (2 sharps: F♯, C♯)
  • Read dotted rhythms and sixteenth notes
  • Understand intervals: unison to octave
  • Recognize tonic triads (I, IV, V chords)
  • Sight-read with wider leaps and more complex rhythms

1 The Musical Alphabet

Music uses 12 unique notes that repeat in higher and lower octaves. Think of them as colors on a palette.

The notes are: C C#/Db D D#/Eb E F F#/Gb G G#/Ab A A#/Bb B

Sharp (#) raises a note by one fret. Flat (b) lowers it by one fret. C# and Db are the same pitch — called "enharmonic equivalents."

Click any key to hear the note!

2 What is a Scale?

A scale is a group of notes arranged in order that sound good together. It's like a "recipe" for creating melodies.

Scales are built using intervals — distances between notes:

  • Half Step (H) = 1 fret on guitar = smallest distance
  • Whole Step (W) = 2 frets on guitar = two half steps

Every scale has a root note (the "home" note) and a specific pattern of whole and half steps.

Think of it like stairs

Some stairs are small (half steps), some are bigger (whole steps). The pattern creates the "flavor" of the scale.

Why learn scales?

Scales train your fingers, ears, and brain. They help you improvise solos, write melodies, understand chords, and navigate the fretboard.

4 The Natural Minor Scale

The Natural Minor Scale has a darker, sadder sound. Formula:

W — H — W — W — H — W — W

Every major scale has a relative minor sharing the same notes:

  • C Major = C D E F G A B C
  • A Minor (relative minor) = A B C D E F G A

To find the relative minor: go down 3 half steps from the major root.

Major = Happy, Bright

"Happy Birthday," "Twinkle Twinkle," most pop songs

Minor = Sad, Dark

"House of the Rising Sun," "Stairway to Heaven," rock ballads

5 What is a Chord?

A chord is three or more notes played together. The simplest chords are triads — three notes.

Triads are built by stacking notes from a scale, skipping every other note:

  • Root (1st) = C
  • Third (3rd) = E (skip D)
  • Fifth (5th) = G (skip F)

So C Major chord = C + E + G. Try it below!

Common Intervals

Unison (0) — Same note
Minor 3rd (3) — Sad sound
Major 3rd (4) — Happy sound
Perfect 5th (7) — Power chord!
Tritone (6) — Devil's interval
Octave (12) — Same note, higher

How to Use

Count semitones (frets) between notes. One fret = one semitone. This lets you build any chord or scale on the fly!

8 How to Practice Effectively

1. Start Slow

Practice at a speed with zero mistakes. Speed comes with accuracy. Use the Metronome!

2. Say Notes Aloud

When playing scales, say each note name. Builds connection between fingers, eyes, ears, and brain.

3. Use Entire Fretboard

Find the same scale in 5 different places. Use the Fretboard tab to explore!

4. Apply to Real Music

Learn songs in the key you're practicing. Theory without application is just math!
